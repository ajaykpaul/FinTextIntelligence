\begin{abstract}
Automated trading and compliance systems read large volumes of headlines, social
posts and press releases, but the text signals they rely on are usually built for one
language and one task, and are rarely checked against the conditions they will be served
under. We present \textbf{FinTextIntelligence}, a benchmark and an end-to-end scoring
service for English and Arabic financial text. We make three contributions. First, we
assemble a bilingual evaluation suite of 15 public sources (121,963 rows after
deduplication) with a fixed, fingerprinted train/dev/test split and an SEC~EDGAR replay
set for claim provenance. Second, we define five tasks, namely financial sentiment,
toxicity (with four subtypes), misleading-content detection, claim provenance and a
trading gate that turns the scores into a buy, sell or review call, and set 14 acceptance
criteria before any results were seen. Third, we train a single XLM-R encoder with four
heads and report every result with bootstrap intervals and multi-seed comparisons. The
released model passes 5 of the 14 criteria: English sentiment (0.807 macro-F1), English
toxicity AUROC (0.953), misleading-content F1 (0.807), provenance precision (72/72) and
claim-extraction recall (0.934). Native Arabic training data lifts Arabic sentiment from
0.455 to 0.734 macro-F1, while Arabic toxicity stays at 0.909 AUROC, limited by a single
dataset. We also show that dynamic int8 quantisation, which the latency budget depended
on, flips up to 9\% of decisions. Code, the evaluation harness and the test cases are
released for research use.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Text moves markets. A headline that a company has cut its guidance, a rumour about a
merger, or a coordinated scam promising guaranteed returns can each change prices or
harm investors within minutes. Desks that trade on news, and compliance teams that
monitor client channels, therefore want an automatic first reading of every item: is it
good or bad news, is it abusive, does it look like a scam or misinformation, and has the
claim been confirmed by a primary source?

Three gaps make this harder than it looks. (i) \emph{Language.} Most financial NLP
resources are English \citep{malo2014good,maia2018www}; Gulf markets need Arabic, where
labelled financial text barely exists. (ii) \emph{Task isolation.} Sentiment, toxicity
and misinformation are usually studied apart, but a trading system has to combine them
into a single decision, and the combination itself can fail. (iii) \emph{Evaluation
practice.} Reported numbers often come from thresholds tuned on the test set, a single
training seed, or a model that differs from the one actually served
\citep{reimers2017reporting,dror2018hitchhiker}.

We address these gaps with FinTextIntelligence (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item A \textbf{bilingual evaluation suite} built from 15 public English and Arabic
  sources, split once with a fixed seed into train, dev and a frozen test set whose
  contents are fingerprinted, plus a provenance replay set built from real SEC 8-K
  filings (\S\ref{sec:data}).
\item \textbf{Five tasks with pre-registered acceptance criteria}: 14 gates covering
  quality, provenance and service level, written before any result (\S\ref{sec:tasks}).
\item A \textbf{shared multilingual model} and a careful evaluation: bootstrap
  intervals, paired comparisons, multi-seed group comparisons, leakage checks and
  served-versus-offline parity (\S\ref{sec:exp}), together with an analysis of where
  and why it fails (\S\ref{sec:analysis}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Financial sentiment.} Financial PhraseBank \citep{malo2014good} and FiQA
\citep{maia2018www} are the standard English benchmarks, and domain-adapted encoders
such as FinBERT \citep{araci2019finbert} improve on general models. A known difficulty is
polarity that depends on the financial object: ``liabilities fell'' is good news. We use
both datasets and test that case explicitly.

\paragraph{Toxicity and offensive language.} English work draws on Jigsaw comments
\citep{jigsaw2018}, Wikipedia Talk \citep{wulczyn2017ex} and HateXplain
\citep{mathew2021hatexplain}. For Arabic, the OSACT shared tasks
\citep{mubarak2020overview,mubarak2022overview} provide offensive-language and hate
labels on tweets, and L-HSAB \citep{mulki2019lhsab} covers Levantine dialect.

\paragraph{Misinformation.} LIAR \citep{wang2017liar}, ISOT \citep{ahmed2017detection},
FakeNewsNet \citep{shu2020fakenewsnet} and PHEME \citep{zubiaga2016analysing} target
political claims, fake news and rumours rather than corporate news, which is a domain gap
we measure qualitatively.

\paragraph{Arabic sentiment.} ArSAS \citep{elmadany2018arsas} and ArSarcasm-v2
\citep{abufarha2021overview} are native-tweet sentiment sets. We use the first for
evaluation and the second for training.

\paragraph{Multilingual encoders and efficiency.} XLM-R \citep{conneau2020unsupervised}
is a strong cross-lingual encoder. Making such models fast on CPUs usually relies on
int8 quantisation \citep{jacob2018quantization} or distillation \citep{sanh2019distilbert};
activation outliers make naive int8 lossy, which motivates methods such as SmoothQuant
\citep{xiao2023smoothquant}.

\paragraph{Evaluation methodology.} We follow the bootstrap tradition for significance
\citep{efron1993introduction,koehn2004statistical,dror2018hitchhiker}, report score
distributions over seeds \citep{reimers2017reporting}, and document the model with a
model card \citep{mitchell2019model}.

\subsection{Splits and fingerprints}
All sources are deduplicated and cut once with a fixed seed into 85,892 train, 18,035 dev
and 18,036 test rows. Dev is used for every choice (thresholds, offsets, seed
selection); test is used only for reporting. Each run's manifest stores a hash of the
actual text in each split, so a later run can prove it was evaluated on exactly the same
rows: every run reported here shares train fingerprint \texttt{6acad74b} and held-out
fingerprint \texttt{23732d3d}.

\subsection{Provenance replay set}
To test claim provenance we built replay cases from real SEC 8-K filings. A
\emph{genuine} claim is the sentence of a company's own Exhibit 99.1 press release that
names it and states the event; a \emph{fabricated} claim swaps the company for one whose
filings cannot support it. The development set (2022-01 to 2024-06) has 125 genuine and
100 fabricated cases; the test set (2024-07 to 2025-12) has 115 and 97, shares no filing
with development, and was scored once.

\subsection{Scam patterns}
Scam-pattern rules (guaranteed returns, urgency plus payment, bank impersonation, and
others) are derived from public SEC investor alerts, with unit tests that quote the
alerts' own phrasing.

\section{Tasks}
\label{sec:tasks}

\paragraph{Task 1: Sentiment.} Three-way (positive / negative / neutral) for EN and
Arabic; gated on English macro-F1, Arabic reported.

\paragraph{Task 2: Toxicity.} Binary toxic / not toxic for EN and AR, plus four reported
subtypes (hate, harassment, profanity, threat). A high-severity lexicon (slurs and direct
threats, with leetspeak folding) acts as a floor and is the only place a rule overrides
the model.

\paragraph{Task 3: Misleading content.} Binary ``likely misleading'' for English. It
detects the style of misleading content and never asserts that a claim is false.

\paragraph{Task 4: Provenance.} For a market-moving claim, extract company, claim type
and particulars, then search an index of primary filings. The state is \emph{pending}
inside a time window and \emph{corroborated} or \emph{uncorroborated} after it.

\paragraph{Task 5: Trading gate.} Combine sentiment, provenance, the misleading flag and
service health into BUY, SELL or REVIEW, with a priority of \emph{now} or
\emph{routine}. The gate never acts on its own: every REVIEW goes to a human queue, and
human decisions are logged as future training labels.

\paragraph{Acceptance criteria.} Fourteen gates were fixed in the requirements before
any result was produced (Table~\ref{tab:main}); three of them (pending-before-expiry,
human-review precision and availability) can only be measured in a live pilot.

\section{Models, Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Model}
One \texttt{xlm-roberta-base} encoder \citep{conneau2020unsupervised} feeds four linear
heads. Training uses 3 epochs, batch 32, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ for heads and
$10^{-5}$ for the encoder, warm-up 6\%, maximum length 256, fp16, a sentiment loss
weight of 2.0 and an Arabic-toxicity weight of 1.5. Training and serving read identical
normalised text, including folding of Arabic spelling variants. The release run took 17
minutes on one A100. For serving, the model is exported to ONNX and run at fp32 with four
CPU threads.

\subsection{Thresholds and protocol}
Toxicity thresholds are tuned recall-first on dev (dev recall $\ge 0.90$, best F1 above
it): 0.645 for English and 0.253 for Arabic. Misleading uses the best dev F1 within
precision and recall floors (0.468). For Arabic sentiment, per-class logit offsets are
tuned on dev. Intervals are 95\% percentile intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of
the test rows, with thresholds held fixed.

\subsection{Arabic sentiment}
Trained only on translated financial sentences, the model scored 0.455 macro-F1 (mean of
four seeds) on native ArSAS tweets and almost never predicted \emph{neutral}

\subsection{Latency and quantisation}
On an Apple M5 CPU the full pipeline's p95 at 256 tokens sits at about 50 ms, landing
either side of the target from session to session (51--61 ms in the release session).
The budget assumed int8 on x86. On a Colab Xeon, default dynamic int8 is $2.2\times$
faster single-threaded, but on 1,000 held-out rows it flips 90 sentiment labels and 41/48
toxicity/misleading flags at the release thresholds, about twice the damage seen on ARM.
Eight recipes were tried (Table~\ref{tab:int8}); those that keep the speed-up damage
decisions, and the most faithful one gains only $1.17\times$.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Arabic toxicity is limited by one dataset.} Per dataset, OSACT4 alone passes
the AUROC gate (0.942) while OSACT5 reaches 0.854 and is underfit even on its training
rows. No threshold on either dataset reaches 0.80 F1 (best 0.760). Several of the most
confident errors are tweets that read as offensive but are labelled not offensive. A
blind 200-tweet relabelling audit is designed and its sample drawn, but needs a fluent
annotator.

\paragraph{F1 targets are threshold ceilings.} The best dev F1 at \emph{any} threshold
is 0.825 for English toxicity and 0.754 for Arabic, both below their targets, so no
decision rule can pass those gates; only a better model or cleaner labels can.

\paragraph{Domain gap in misleading content.} The head learnt ``misleading'' from
political claims and fake news. On ordinary corporate news it over-flags: ``Revenue
missed consensus and the board cut its full-year guidance'' scores 0.52 and a routine
stake acquisition by a bank scores 0.83. The gate sends both to a person, which is safe
but noisy.

\paragraph{Polarity of financial objects.} ``Liabilities fell sharply in the third
quarter as the group repaid debt'' is scored positive (0.93), the correct reading, and
gives BUY. The Arabic counterparts of simple profit and loss headlines are scored
correctly (0.98 positive, 0.96 negative).

\paragraph{Gate composition.} Because the gate does not consult toxicity, an abusive post
with negative tone yields SELL (0.85) under the trading profile. Such content should be
routed to the compliance profile; we report this as a design limitation rather than a
model error.

\paragraph{Served path.} A late check found the default configuration still held the
rule-based scorer's thresholds (0.35 instead of 0.645 for English toxicity). Score-level
parity tests had passed because they did not compare final flags. A release
configuration and a test that fails on threshold drift now guard this.

\section{Conclusion and Future Directions}

FinTextIntelligence combines four text classifiers, two rule layers, a retrieval-based
provenance check and a trading gate behind one service for English and Arabic financial
text, and evaluates them against criteria fixed in advance. The release model passes 5 of
14 gates and is not accepted, but the failures are well characterised: Arabic toxicity
is bounded by one dataset and possibly its labels, toxicity F1 targets lie above the
threshold ceiling, and the latency gap needs a smaller model or different hardware rather
than more tuning. The most useful lessons are methodological: use several seeds from
the first comparison, test the whole served path including its configuration, and
measure flipped decisions rather than score drift.

Next steps are a six-layer distilled encoder for latency, the Arabic label audit,
continued pre-training on unlabelled Arabic tweets, outlier-aware quantisation, and a
hand-labelled in-domain test set of issuer and bank-channel text.

\section*{Limitations}
Every quality number comes from public benchmarks (tweets, Wikipedia comments, news),
none of which is financial text from the intended channels, so the in-domain error rate
is unknown. Misleading-content detection is English only. Arabic sentiment is measured
on dialectal tweets rather than Arabic financial text, and its training data is partly
machine-translated. Latency was measured on a laptop CPU, not on the reference server.
Three acceptance criteria require a live pilot. The trading gate gives direction only,
not size or timing, and ignores toxicity.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
The service reports and people decide: no score blocks content or executes a trade on
its own, and every uncertain item goes to a human reviewer. Personal data (phone numbers,
emails) is scrubbed before scoring. No private, customer or employee data was used;
every source is public and its licence is recorded, and captured production text is
excluded from training until cleared. Toxicity labels encode annotator judgements that
may be noisy or culturally biased, particularly across Arabic dialects. The trained
weights inherit non-commercial terms from some training data and are shared for research
and evaluation only.

\section{Evaluation Metrics and Statistics}

Sentiment uses macro-F1 over three classes; toxicity reports AUROC and F1 at the
dev-tuned threshold; misleading content reports F1. Intervals are 95\% percentile
intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the 18,036 test rows; the whole test set is
resampled together, so the EN--AR gap interval is paired. Comparisons between two runs
resample the rows once per draw and score both runs on the same draw, reporting the
interval on $B - A$; the call is ``no detectable difference'' when it contains zero. The
tool refuses to pair runs that were not tested on the same rows, checked by a per-row
digest. Comparisons between setups resample test rows and runs within each setup
together, so the interval covers both test-sample and seed variation. Provenance
precision lower bounds are lower ends of 95\% Wilson score intervals.
